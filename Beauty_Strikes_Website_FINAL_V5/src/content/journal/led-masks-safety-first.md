LED masks have moved from dermatology offices to bathroom shelves. They can be a reasonable addition to a routine — but they are not magic, and they are not risk-free. Here's what to know first.

## How LED light therapy works

LED devices shine low-energy light at specific wavelengths onto the skin. Unlike lasers, they don't heat or damage the skin's surface. The idea is that light at certain wavelengths interacts with cells in ways that may support repair or reduce bacteria.

The colors you'll see most often:

| Light | Typical wavelength | Commonly marketed for |
|---|---|---|
| Red | around 630–660 nm | Fine lines, skin texture, recovery |
| Near-infrared | around 830–850 nm (invisible) | Often combined with red light |
| Blue | around 415 nm | Mild to moderate acne |

## What the evidence realistically supports

Research on LED therapy exists, but much of it uses small study groups, short timeframes, and professional devices that may be more powerful than home versions. Reported effects for red and near-infrared light include modest improvements in skin texture and fine lines. Blue light has some evidence for reducing mild acne.

> Realistic expectation: subtle, gradual improvement with consistent use over weeks — not the dramatic before-and-after images used in advertising.

## Safety checks before you buy

- **Regulatory status.** For the US market, check whether the manufacturer states FDA clearance for that specific device, and for which use. Clearance for one indication doesn't cover every claim on the box.
- **Stated wavelengths.** A trustworthy brand lists them. Vague claims like "medical-grade light" without numbers are a warning sign.
- **Eye protection.** Bright LEDs close to the eyes can be uncomfortable or harmful. Check whether the mask includes eye shields or requires goggles, and follow the instructions.
- **Instructions and treatment time.** Longer or more frequent sessions than the manufacturer recommends are not better.

## Who should talk to a doctor first

Ask a dermatologist or doctor before using an LED mask if you:

- take medications that increase light sensitivity,
- have a photosensitive condition or eye condition,
- have a history of seizures triggered by light,
- are pregnant,
- have an active skin condition or are being treated for skin cancer.

## How to use one sensibly

1. Start with clean, dry skin unless the instructions say otherwise.
2. Keep sessions to the recommended length and frequency.
3. Stop if you notice persistent redness, eye strain or headaches.
4. Keep using sunscreen and your regular routine — LED therapy doesn't replace either.

## The bottom line

An LED mask can be a pleasant, low-effort habit with modest benefits. Buy from a brand that lists wavelengths and safety information clearly, follow the instructions exactly, and keep your expectations grounded.
