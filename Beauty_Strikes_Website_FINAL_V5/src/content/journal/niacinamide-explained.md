Niacinamide shows up in serums, toners, moisturizers and even sunscreens. It is popular for a good reason: it is well tolerated by most skin types and has a reasonable body of research behind it. It is also wildly over-marketed. This guide separates the two.

## What niacinamide is

Niacinamide (also called nicotinamide) is a form of vitamin B3. In skincare it is water-soluble, stable in most formulas, and easy to combine with other ingredients. That stability is a big part of why formulators like it.

## What the research supports

Topical niacinamide has been studied in a number of small and mid-sized clinical studies. The results most often reported are:

- **Barrier support.** Niacinamide is associated with increased production of ceramides and other barrier lipids, which can help skin hold on to water.
- **Oil and shine.** Some studies found reduced sebum production with regular use, though results vary between people.
- **Uneven tone.** Niacinamide appears to reduce the transfer of pigment to skin cells. Studies using around 5% have reported a visible reduction in hyperpigmentation over several weeks.
- **Redness and texture.** Improvements in redness, blotchiness and the appearance of fine lines have been reported, usually modest.

> Realistic expectation: niacinamide is a helpful supporting ingredient, not a fix for acne, melasma or deep wrinkles. Results build over weeks, and they are usually subtle.

## Typical concentrations

Most research uses concentrations between **2% and 5%**. Many products now advertise 10% or more. Higher percentages are not proven to work better, and some people find them more irritating — redness, itching or a warm, flushed feeling.

If you are new to niacinamide, a product in the 2–5% range is a sensible starting point.

## How to use it

1. **Where it goes.** Apply after cleansing and before moisturizer. In a serum or toner, it is usually the first or second layer.
2. **How often.** Once or twice a day. Morning use pairs well with sunscreen.
3. **Give it time.** Assess after six to eight weeks, not after three days.
4. **Patch test.** Try it on a small area for a few days before using it on your whole face, especially at higher concentrations.

## Can you combine it with vitamin C?

This is one of the most persistent skincare myths. The concern came from older research showing that niacinamide and ascorbic acid can react under heat. Under normal room temperature and in modern formulations, this is generally not considered a practical problem. If your skin feels irritated when you layer them, use one in the morning and the other in the evening.

Niacinamide also combines well with hyaluronic acid, ceramides, retinoids and sunscreen.

## Who should be careful

- People with very reactive skin should start with low concentrations.
- If you notice persistent stinging or flushing, stop and simplify your routine.
- Niacinamide is not a substitute for medical treatment of acne, rosacea or pigmentation disorders.

## The bottom line

Niacinamide earns its place as a low-risk, multi-purpose ingredient. Choose a well-formulated product in the 2–5% range, use it consistently, and judge it by gradual changes rather than marketing promises.
